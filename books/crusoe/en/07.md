**Chapter Seven. Brazil, and the First of September**

<!-- s002 -->
Four years in Brazil made me a farmer, which is the last thing anybody would have expected, including me.

<!-- s003 -->
I bought poor land because it was what I could afford, and I planted tobacco because it was what the poor land would take. For the first two years I ate rice and worked until dark. I owed money to a man in the town who was patient with me for reasons I never understood.

<!-- s004 -->
The third year the crop was good. The fourth year it was better.

<!-- s005 -->
By the fifth I had a house with a floor in it, two men working with me for wages, and a neighbour on either side.

<!-- s006 -->
I was twenty-six, and solvent, and about as far from the sea as a man can get while still being able to smell it.

<!-- s007 -->
I have gone over that year many times, and I cannot find the moment where it went wrong, because nothing went wrong. That is the difficulty. Everything was fine, and I ruined it anyway.

<!-- s008 -->
![About as far from the sea as a man can get while still being able to smell it.](media/ch07-a.jpg)

<!-- s009 -->
My neighbours came to me in the winter with a proposition.

<!-- s010 -->
They had done their arithmetic. Field hands were dear in Brazil because the trade was licensed and the licence-holders took their cut. But a ship going quietly down to the Guinea coast, fitted out by three or four planters between them, could bring people back for their own estates and pay nothing to anybody.

<!-- s011 -->
They wanted my share of the money and they wanted me aboard, because I knew that coast, and had a little Arabic, and had been down there twice.

<!-- s012 -->
They put it to me across my own table, over my own wine, and they were not wicked men. That is the thing I have never been able to make anyone understand. They were my neighbours. One of them had sat up two nights with me when I had the fever. They spoke about it the way men speak about buying oxen, and they meant no harm by it, and they would have been genuinely puzzled to be told there was any harm in it to mean.

<!-- s013 -->
I said no.

<!-- s014 -->
I said it plainly and I did not soften it, and one of them was angry and one of them laughed, and the third looked at me afterwards in a way I did not like at all.

<!-- s015 -->
I have never once regretted that no.

<!-- s016 -->
⁂

<!-- s017 -->
But here is what happened next, and it is the reason I am writing any of this down.

<!-- s018 -->
Two months later a different offer came. A ship going up the coast for hides and dye-wood and whatever else was going. Ordinary cargo. Ordinary trade. No people in the hold.

<!-- s019 -->
They wanted a supercargo who could keep the books.

<!-- s020 -->
And I said yes.

<!-- s021 -->
Why? I have had a great deal of time to answer that, and the honest answer is small and shabby. My tobacco was doing well and it was doing well slowly. The voyage would make in five months what my fields would make in three years.

<!-- s022 -->
And under that, further down where I did not look at it: I was bored. I was twenty-six and I had a house with a floor and two men working for wages, and every morning was the same morning, and somewhere out past the river mouth there were ships going somewhere.

<!-- s023 -->
The same want that walked me down to Hull at eighteen was sitting in my chest at twenty-six with its boots off, perfectly comfortable, waiting.

<!-- s024 -->
⁂

<!-- s025 -->
I want to put the two things side by side, because for years I refused to.

<!-- s026 -->
I had turned down a fortune rather than buy a human being, and I was proud of it, and I was right to be.

<!-- s027 -->
Then I signed for a cargo of hides because I was restless. I left my fields to a manager. I did not tell my mother I was going, and I did not write to my father. He had not had a letter from me in eight years. He was, though I did not know it, already dead.

<!-- s028 -->
A man can be very brave about the enormous thing and never once do the small dull one.

<!-- s029 -->
The small dull one, in my case, was: *stay where you are and write a letter.*

<!-- s030 -->
⁂

<!-- s031 -->
We sailed on the first of September, in the year 1659.

<!-- s032 -->
I did not notice the date until we were three days out and I was writing up the book. Then I sat looking at the figures for a while.

<!-- s033 -->
The first of September, 1651, I had gone down the Humber with my father's rope on my arm.

<!-- s034 -->
Eight years to the day.

<!-- s035 -->
I am not a superstitious man. I have never believed the sea keeps accounts. But I will say that I did not like it, and that I did not write it in the book, and that I have never since been able to see that date without something turning over in me.

<!-- s036 -->
![She hit a bar with the whole weight of herself and stopped dead.](media/ch07-b.jpg)

<!-- s037 -->
The storm took us on the twelfth day and it did not let go for eleven.

<!-- s038 -->
It drove us west and then north-west, out of any water we knew, past the point where the master could tell me within two hundred miles where we were. We lost the mainmast on the fourth day and a man with it. We lost the boat on the sixth. After that we were simply a thing being pushed.

<!-- s039 -->
On the eleventh morning a man at the head shouted the word every one of us had been waiting eleven days to hear and dreading equally.

<!-- s040 -->
Land.

<!-- s041 -->
Then, almost at once, we struck.

<!-- s042 -->
⁂

<!-- s043 -->
She hit a bar with the whole weight of herself and stopped dead, and the sea began to break clean over her, and I understood in about four seconds that the ship was finished and that we had perhaps an hour.

<!-- s044 -->
We got the small boat over. Eleven of us in it.

<!-- s045 -->
I do not remember getting in. I remember that I had gone below one last time and come up with almost nothing sensible. No water. No bread. What I had done, without thinking about it at all, was take my father's rope off the peg where I kept it and put it over my head and one shoulder.

<!-- s046 -->
Twenty feet of good hemp, well laid, eight years old and still sound.

<!-- s047 -->
Then we were in the boat and away, and the sea took us the way a hand sweeps crumbs off a table.

<!-- s048 -->
⁂

<!-- s049 -->
A wave came in behind us that I did not see, and I have always been glad I did not see it.

<!-- s050 -->
The boat was simply gone. There was no capsizing, no shouting, nothing you could describe as an event. One moment there were eleven men in a boat and the next there was water, and I was under it, and being rolled along the bottom hard enough that I have marks on my back to this day.

<!-- s051 -->
I came up and got one breath.

<!-- s052 -->
The next one took me in again, and this time it carried me a long way, and when it put me down my knees hit sand.

<!-- s053 -->
I got up and ran. The next one caught me at the shoulders, knocked me flat and dragged me back. I got up and ran again. I did that four times. The fourth time I got up, the water only came to my ankles. I went twenty steps further and lay down on the wet sand and could not move at all.

<!-- s054 -->
![The first night of twenty-eight years.](media/ch07-c.jpg)

<!-- s055 -->
I lay there a long while. Then it got dark, and I made myself get up, because of the tide and because of whatever might live there.

<!-- s056 -->
There was fresh water in a hollow half a mile inland and I drank until I was sick.

<!-- s057 -->
Then I climbed a tree, because I could think of nothing else to do, and I wedged myself in a fork with my father's rope round the trunk and round my waist and knotted, so that I would not fall out when I slept.

<!-- s058 -->
That is the first thing that rope did for me on that island. It was not the last.

<!-- s059 -->
I hung in the dark and listened to the sea taking my ship apart three hundred yards away, and I did the only arithmetic that was left to do.

<!-- s060 -->
Eleven men had gone into the boat.

<!-- s061 -->
I could see the whole beach in the moonlight, both ways, and there was nothing on it but weed.

<!-- s062 -->
I want to write down exactly what I said out loud, because I have never forgotten it and because it was not a prayer, whatever it sounds like.

<!-- s063 -->
I said: "There is nobody left to blame but me."

<!-- s064 -->
And then I slept, hanging in a tree, on the first night of twenty-eight years.
