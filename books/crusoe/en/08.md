<!-- s001 -->
**Chapter Eight. What the Sea Gave Back**

<!-- s002 -->
In the morning the ship was still there.

<!-- s003 -->
The tide had gone out and left her sitting on the bar with a list on her, about a mile off, whole enough that I could see the stump of the mainmast and a rag of canvas moving.

<!-- s004 -->
I sat on the sand and looked at her for a long time, and I felt something I am not proud of, which was fury.

<!-- s005 -->
If we had stayed aboard, all eleven of us would have walked off her dry at low water.

<!-- s006 -->
Every man in that boat was dead because we had been frightened at the wrong hour. I have seen men decide well and die anyway, since. But that morning I sat on a beach and understood that fear is not a warning. Fear is a noise, and sometimes it is a noise about nothing, and it had just drowned ten men.

<!-- s007 -->
Then I stopped thinking about it, because the tide turns twice a day and there is only so much daylight.

<!-- s008 -->
![I made twelve trips over the next eleven days.](media/ch08-a.jpg)

<!-- s009 -->
I swam out.

<!-- s010 -->
I will not pretend it was brave. It was a mile of flat water and I was twenty-seven and I had nothing else to do that day.

<!-- s011 -->
Getting up her side was the hard part, and it was the rope that did it — I threw the coil until a fluke of the broken forechains caught it, and went up hand over hand with my feet on the planking.

<!-- s012 -->
Then I stood on the deck of a dead ship with the water sloshing about below and I began, God forgive me, to feel almost cheerful.

<!-- s013 -->
Because she was full.

<!-- s014 -->
⁂

<!-- s015 -->
I made twelve trips over the next eleven days.

<!-- s016 -->
The first day I built a raft out of spare topmasts and planks, lashed with — well, with everything that would lash, and I want to be exact here, because people always ask about the rope.

<!-- s017 -->
My father's twenty feet were not enough to build a raft. Of course they were not. There were coils of proper cordage in that ship, hundreds of fathoms of it, and that is what the raft was built with.

<!-- s018 -->
What the twenty feet did was get me aboard the first time.

<!-- s019 -->
Everything else came from that.

<!-- s020 -->
I have thought a great deal about how the small thing you happen to be carrying decides whether you ever reach the big thing you need.

<!-- s021 -->
⁂

<!-- s022 -->
I will not write out a list of what I took. Lists are the death of a story and I have read books that were nothing else.

<!-- s023 -->
But there were four things that mattered, and I will name them.

<!-- s024 -->
The carpenter's chest, which was worth more than everything else together and which I nearly left behind on the second trip because it was heavy.

<!-- s025 -->
Two guns, a fowling piece, and all the powder — the powder in three lots, kept in three places afterwards, because a man with all his powder in one cave is one lightning strike from being an animal again.

<!-- s026 -->
Bread, rice, three cheeses, five pieces of dried goat, and a little grain that I thought was worthless and was not.

<!-- s027 -->
And a small chest of tools and instruments out of the master's cabin. There was a quadrant in it, broken. Two knives. A pair of scissors. Pens, ink dried in the pot, and paper.

<!-- s028 -->
I sat on the sand that evening with the paper in my hands.

<!-- s029 -->
Nobody was ever going to read it. I knew that quite clearly by then.

<!-- s030 -->
I kept it anyway, and it is why you are reading this at all, so make of that what you like.

<!-- s031 -->
⁂

<!-- s032 -->
On the thirteenth day the wind came round to the north-east and blew hard for two nights, and in the morning the bar was empty.

<!-- s033 -->
She had gone in the dark, without a sound I could hear over the surf, in the same quiet way the ship in Yarmouth Roads had gone eight years before.

<!-- s034 -->
I stood on the beach and looked at the empty place.

<!-- s035 -->
I had everything off her that a man could get, and I still stood there for the better part of an hour. When I walked away I understood what I had just watched: the last piece of the world I came from going under water.

<!-- s036 -->
![In the morning the bar was empty.](media/ch08-b.jpg)

<!-- s037 -->
It took me nine days to learn that I was on an island.

<!-- s038 -->
I climbed the hill in the middle on the fourth day and I could see water on three sides, and I told myself the fourth side might go on for ever, and I spent five more days walking until I proved that it did not.

<!-- s039 -->
I sat down when I proved it. That is the honest word for what I did. My legs stopped holding me up and I sat down in the grass.

<!-- s040 -->
Eleven miles long. Perhaps five across at the wide end. Goats on the high ground. Birds. No people, no smoke, no path.

<!-- s041 -->
I made myself say it out loud, because a thing said out loud can be worked with and a thing kept in the chest cannot:

<!-- s042 -->
"I am the only man on this island, and there is no ship, and nobody is coming."

<!-- s043 -->
Then I got up and walked back to my tent, because it was going to rain.

<!-- s044 -->
⁂

<!-- s045 -->
I found the grave in the eleventh week.

<!-- s046 -->
I was cutting up towards the high ground after goats, along the top of a small valley on the north side where a stream came down. I came out onto a flat shelf of grass above the water. It was the best place on the whole island to put a house, which is what made me stop.

<!-- s047 -->
There was a low heap of stones on it. Man-high, and half again as long. Too neat for weather to have made.

<!-- s048 -->
I stood looking at it for a while with my heart going.

<!-- s049 -->
At the head of it, set upright and wedged with smaller stones, there was a flat slab about the size of a book, and there was writing cut into it.

<!-- s050 -->
I got down on my knees in the wet grass and turned my head sideways, because the light was low and coming from the wrong side.

<!-- s051 -->
And I read it.

<!-- s052 -->
⁂

<!-- s053 -->
I want to be very careful with what I say next, because it is the hinge of my whole life and I have seen people make it sentimental.

<!-- s054 -->
The writing was Arabic.

<!-- s055 -->
I could read it.

<!-- s056 -->
I could read it because for two years I had been the property of a man in Salé. I had learned his language out of the mouths of the people who owned me. I had been taught my letters by a boy of thirteen, who rubbed his own name out of the dust with his foot so that nobody would see.

<!-- s057 -->
I had spent eight years thinking of those two years as the hole in the middle of my life. The thing that had been taken from me. The part I would not talk about.

<!-- s058 -->
And then I knelt on a shelf of grass on an island with no name, four thousand miles from that garden, in front of a heap of stones. And it turned out that the only thing in my whole life that let me understand what was written there was the worst thing that had ever happened to me.

<!-- s059 -->
I sat back on my heels and I laughed, and then I did not laugh.

<!-- s060 -->
![The writing was Arabic. I could read it.](media/ch08-c.jpg)

<!-- s061 -->
The stone said three things.

<!-- s062 -->
It said a name: *Yusuf ibn Abdullah.*

<!-- s063 -->
It said, under the name, the plainest sentence a man can cut into rock, and I will give it here in English as near as I can put it: *he came from the sea, and he was alone, and here he is buried.*

<!-- s064 -->
And under that there was a date.

<!-- s065 -->
It was written in the numerals I had learned in Salé, and I could read every figure of it perfectly well, and it meant nothing to me at all — because it was not counted from the year I counted from. It was a year in the other reckoning, the one my master's household used, and I had never once bothered to learn how the two lined up.

<!-- s066 -->
I sat with my hand flat on that stone until it got dark.

<!-- s067 -->
Somebody had carved it. That was the thing I could not get past.

<!-- s068 -->
A man cannot cut his own headstone and then lie down under a heap of stones and pull them over himself. Somebody had done it for him.

<!-- s069 -->
So there had been two.

<!-- s070 -->
And whoever the second one was, he had cut the letters, and laid the stones, and then walked away down this same valley — and there was nothing else on this island. No hut. No wall. No ashes.

<!-- s071 -->
Which meant that either he had got off, somehow, from an island that nobody comes to.

<!-- s072 -->
Or he had not, and I had simply not found him yet.
