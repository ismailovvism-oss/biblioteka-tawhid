<!-- s001 -->
**Chapter Twelve. The Ship, and the Door**

<!-- s002 -->
There were eleven men in that boat and three of them were tied.

<!-- s003 -->
We lay in the scrub above the beach and watched them come in, and I understood the whole business inside two minutes, because I had spent five years at sea and a lifetime keeping other men's accounts.

<!-- s004 -->
Eight armed men. Three bound. And the bound ones were being put ashore, not killed, which meant nobody wanted the blame.

<!-- s005 -->
It was a mutiny, and the ship out there was in the wrong hands.

<!-- s006 -->
![Mostly by shouting the right names in the dark.](media/ch12-a.jpg)

<!-- s007 -->
I am not going to make a battle of the next two days either.

<!-- s008 -->
We had thirty-five years of that island and they had a beach and no water and no idea that anything on it could think. I knew every stone. Friday could run further in an hour than they could walk in three.

<!-- s009 -->
We split them. We took their boat in the dark. We let them wander half a day in the heat and shout to each other, and by the time we walked out with four guns and a voice they had not heard in their lives, two of them sat straight down in the sand.

<!-- s010 -->
Nobody died. I want that in the record, because at forty-four I had wanted a reason to kill somebody on a beach, and at fifty-five I got one and did not take it.

<!-- s011 -->
The captain was one of the bound men. He had commanded that ship for nine years and his own mate had taken it off him.

<!-- s012 -->
He kept looking at my goatskin coat and my hair, which was down my back, and at the two posts outside my door, and finally he asked me straight out how long.

<!-- s013 -->
I said, "Thirty-five years and four months."

<!-- s014 -->
He sat down on the ground. He was not a young man either.

<!-- s015 -->
⁂

<!-- s016 -->
We took the ship back on the third night. The captain and Friday and I went out in the boat with five men who had never wanted the mutiny in the first place, and it was over in about ten minutes, mostly by shouting the right names in the dark.

<!-- s017 -->
And then it was done, and there was a ship at anchor off my island with my name known aboard her, and I was going home.

<!-- s018 -->
I had waited thirty-five years for that sentence. I went and sat on the sand by myself for an hour and could not feel anything at all.

<!-- s019 -->
⁂

<!-- s020 -->
There were five men left who could not be taken to England, because in England there is a rope waiting for men who take a ship.

<!-- s021 -->
The captain would have hanged them there and been within his rights. I asked for them instead.

<!-- s022 -->
I gave them the island.

<!-- s023 -->
I want to set down exactly what I left, because that island is not a story to me, it is a place, and somebody is standing in it now.

<!-- s024 -->
I left them the house with the double wall and the second way out. I left the fenced ground, the two big fields and the five small hidden ones, and eleven years of seed. I left the herd — forty-odd head by then, split in three as I had kept them.

<!-- s025 -->
I left the tools out of the carpenter's chest, and both remaining guns and what powder there was, in three places, and I told them why it was in three places.

<!-- s026 -->
I left a paper with everything on it. Which crab not to eat after the rains. Where the seep is in the north valley in a dry year. How the boats used to come, and from where, and that they had not come for eleven years, and that eleven years is not for ever.

<!-- s027 -->
And I left the account you are reading, or the first draft of it, sealed in a jar under the floor, in case nobody ever came for them either.

<!-- s028 -->
⁂

<!-- s029 -->
Two things I did on the last morning, alone, before the boat came in.

<!-- s030 -->
I went up the valley to the shelf of grass and swept the flat stone with my hand, the way I had four times a year for nineteen years, and I said the words for a man you are leaving.

<!-- s031 -->
Then I went to the overhang, and I put the astrolabe back where I had found it, wrapped in a piece of good cloth this time, in the same fold of rock.

<!-- s032 -->
I had thought about taking it. I had used it for nineteen years and it had given me my direction and my noons and the only certainty I owned.

<!-- s033 -->
But it was his. He came out of the sea with it in his pocket, and he never got off, and the least I could do was leave the next man the same chance he had left me.

<!-- s034 -->
If you are ever on that island, it is sixty paces west of the stones, at the back, on the right hand as you come in under the rock.

<!-- s035 -->
⁂

<!-- s036 -->
Friday came with me. I want to be exact about this because it matters.

<!-- s037 -->
I asked him. I asked him twice, on two different days, and the second time I told him plainly that I could put him ashore on his own coast on the way north, and that the ship would do it because the captain owed me a ship.

<!-- s038 -->
He thought for two days.

<!-- s039 -->
Then he said he would come, and that he wanted to see a place where there were ten thousand people, because he did not believe me about it.

<!-- s040 -->
He came. He was disappointed by London, which he said smelled worse than the beach with the bones, and he was right.

<!-- s041 -->
![The same three words, once a year, on the same date.](media/ch12-b.jpg)

<!-- s042 -->
We came into England in the summer of 1687.

<!-- s043 -->
I had gone down the Humber on the first of September 1651, eighteen years old, with twenty feet of stolen rope on my arm.

<!-- s044 -->
I came back thirty-six years later, fifty-four years old, grey to the shoulders, with a man from the other side of the world beside me who had never seen a horse.

<!-- s045 -->
My mother was alive.

<!-- s046 -->
She was eighty-one and nearly blind and she was living with my sister's family outside York, and when they brought me in she put both hands flat on my face and went over it the way you read something in the dark.

<!-- s047 -->
Then she said, "You have your father's jaw."

<!-- s048 -->
I had forty minutes with her before she was tired. I had another eleven months after that, and I did not waste one week of them, and I was there at the end.

<!-- s049 -->
That is the happiest sentence in this book and I have put it in the middle of a paragraph on purpose, because that is where it happened.

<!-- s050 -->
⁂

<!-- s051 -->
My father had died in the ninth year.

<!-- s052 -->
I knew it before anybody told me, because they took me to the storehouse first — it was still there, still selling rope, run by a man who had been a boy sweeping it when I left.

<!-- s053 -->
He gave me the ledgers.

<!-- s054 -->
And in the back of the current one, on the last ruled page, there was a column that had nothing to do with rope.

<!-- s055 -->
**1 September 1652 — not returned.**

<!-- s056 -->
**1 September 1653 — not returned.**

<!-- s057 -->
It went down the page in my father's small square hand. The same three words, once a year, on the same date.

<!-- s058 -->
Eight of them.

<!-- s059 -->
Then the hand changed, and the ninth was in a rounder, younger writing, and it said the same three words, and so did every one after it, all the way down that page and onto a second one, thirty-six entries in all, the last of them written ten months before I walked through the door.

<!-- s060 -->
I asked the man who kept the shop about it.

<!-- s061 -->
He said my father had given him one instruction when he took over the books, and had made him repeat it back, and that he had never once asked whether it was sensible.

<!-- s062 -->
*Every first of September. Same words. Do not stop.*

<!-- s063 -->
⁂

<!-- s064 -->
Now I will say the useful thing, once, and then I will finish.

<!-- s065 -->
I was offered a fortune to sell a boy and I would not do it, and I was right, and I have never regretted it for an hour.

<!-- s066 -->
But I could not walk four days from Yarmouth to York.

<!-- s067 -->
I could not write one letter in eight years.

<!-- s068 -->
I could refuse the enormous wrong in front of witnesses and I could not do the small dull right thing in an empty room, and it cost me thirty-five years on a beach, and it cost my father every first of September for the rest of his life.

<!-- s069 -->
If you are young and you are reading this, that is the whole of what I have to tell you, and it is not about islands.

<!-- s070 -->
The large virtues are easy. They come with an audience and they feel wonderful.

<!-- s071 -->
Go and do the small one. The one nobody will see. The one you gave your word about.

<!-- s072 -->
⁂

<!-- s073 -->
My estate in Brazil had been kept for me the whole time by two honest men and a monastery, which is a thing I still cannot entirely believe, and it made me rich past any use I had for it.

<!-- s074 -->
I found Xury in Lisbon in the spring of 1689.

<!-- s075 -->
He was a mate with a certificate and a scar across his right hand and a good deal of grey in his beard, and he looked at me for four seconds before he knew me.

<!-- s076 -->
Then he said the first thing he had said to me in thirty years. He had heard how I lost a ship on a bar in the Caribbee. It did not surprise him in the least, because I had never once in my life properly watched the set of a current.

<!-- s077 -->
We had eleven days in that city. I have had worse years.

<!-- s078 -->
![I raised my hand.](media/ch12-c.jpg)

<!-- s079 -->
Last of all I went to Hull.

<!-- s080 -->
I walked out to the end of the pier on a grey morning in October and stood where the fishermen stand, and where somebody had stood on the first of September 1651.

<!-- s081 -->
There was a ship going out on the tide.

<!-- s082 -->
There was a boy at her rail, about eighteen, looking back at the town the way you look at something you are pleased to be finished with.

<!-- s083 -->
I raised my hand.

<!-- s084 -->
He did not wave. Of course he did not wave. I was a stroke of dark against the grey, no taller than his thumbnail, and there are fishermen out there every morning of the year.

<!-- s085 -->
I stood there until the shape of him went into the haze, and I found that I was not sad, or not only sad.

<!-- s086 -->
I was fifty-six years old and I had a mother's grave to keep and a friend in Lisbon and a man in my house who could read and reckon and outrun a horse.

<!-- s087 -->
And I had an island.

<!-- s088 -->
There are five men on it, and forty goats, and eleven years of seed, and a paper telling them which crab not to eat. There is a heap of stones on a shelf of grass above a stream, with a name cut into it, and a piece of green brass sixty paces west of it under a rock.

<!-- s089 -->
They will be wanting things by now. Nails. Cloth. A woman or two would not go amiss, if any would go, and I have money enough to make it worth their while.

<!-- s090 -->
I have been reckoning the passage all autumn, on paper, in columns, in my father's small square hand.

<!-- s091 -->
It is not a difficult voyage if a man watches the set of the current.
