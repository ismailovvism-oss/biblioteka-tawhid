<!-- s001 -->
**Chapter Nine. The Star and the Bread**

<!-- s002 -->
The fever came in the second summer and it very nearly finished me.

<!-- s003 -->
It began with cold, which is an absurd thing to feel in that country. I sat by a fire in the middle of the afternoon in a shirt and two goatskins and shook until my teeth hurt.

<!-- s004 -->
Then the heat came, and after the heat, nothing much for a day and a half.

<!-- s005 -->
I have no clear memory of most of it. What I remember is one afternoon of it, and I remember that afternoon better than I remember whole years.

<!-- s006 -->
![I could not cross four feet.](media/ch09-a.jpg)

<!-- s007 -->
I woke on my side. I could see the water jar. It was about four feet from my hand.

<!-- s008 -->
I could not reach it.

<!-- s009 -->
I understood the whole of my situation in about the time it takes to read this sentence. If I could not cross four feet, I was going to die of thirst inside a hut, with water in the room, on an island with fruit and goats and a stream, because there was nobody to push a jar six inches.

<!-- s010 -->
Not the sea. Not the cannibals I had not yet heard of. Six inches.

<!-- s011 -->
I got to the jar in the end. It took a long time and I will not describe it.

<!-- s012 -->
But I lay there afterwards with the water going down my chin and I said something out loud, and it was the first honest thing I had said in eleven years.

<!-- s013 -->
I said, "I cannot do this by myself."

<!-- s014 -->
⁂

<!-- s015 -->
I want to stop here and be careful, because this is the part of the account where a man is expected to tell you that the heavens opened.

<!-- s016 -->
Nothing opened.

<!-- s017 -->
I got better over about nine days. I ate. I mended the hut where the rain had come in. And the sentence I had said sat there in the middle of my head like a stone in a boot, and would not be walked off.

<!-- s018 -->
Because I had known it was true for a long time and had never said it.

<!-- s019 -->
Everything I had was luck. The ship on the bar. The rope that caught the chains. The grain I thought was rubbish. Ten men in a boat and one on a beach, and the one on the beach was not the strongest or the best.

<!-- s020 -->
I had spent eleven years congratulating myself on being ingenious.

<!-- s021 -->
Sitting there mending a roof, I could not any longer make the accounts balance. There was too much on the credit side that I had not put there.

<!-- s022 -->
⁂

<!-- s023 -->
So I decided.

<!-- s024 -->
I am not going to write out how I got from that to what I did next. It took four months, and most of it happened in my hands rather than my head — while I was walking, while I was mending nets, while I was lying awake.

<!-- s025 -->
I will only say this. I had lived two years in a house where a man went out into a wet garden with a fever because it was the hour. At the time I told myself I was watching a prisoner's routine. I had been wrong. I had been watching a free man.

<!-- s026 -->
And I will say that the first time I tried it, I knelt down on the sand and got as far as putting my hands on my knees and then stopped, because I had realised something ridiculous.

<!-- s027 -->
I did not know which way to face.

<!-- s028 -->
⁂

<!-- s029 -->
Here is what I actually knew.

<!-- s030 -->
Mecca lies a long way east of Salé, and somewhat south. I knew that because in that house you cannot help knowing it — the whole building was arranged around it.

<!-- s031 -->
I knew I was a very long way west of Salé, and I knew, from the wreck's reckoning before the storm, roughly how far south.

<!-- s032 -->
What I did not know was where I was now, and how much of the world lay between.

<!-- s033 -->
I sat on the sand and laughed at myself, and then I went and got the broken quadrant out of the chest.

<!-- s034 -->
It was broken. Of course it was broken. It had been broken for eleven years and I had kept it because I keep everything.

<!-- s035 -->
⁂

<!-- s036 -->
Which is when I remembered the shelf of grass.

<!-- s037 -->
It took me two days to find what I was looking for, and I found it about sixty paces from the heap of stones, under an overhang where the rock came out over the stream.

<!-- s038 -->
Somebody had lived there. Not built — lived. There was a flat place worn in the floor. There was a ring of stones black on the inside. There was a fish-hook made out of a nail.

<!-- s039 -->
And in a fold of rock at the back there was something wrapped in the rotted remains of what had once been cloth. It was a disc of brass about the size of my palm, green all over, with a ring at the top and marks around the rim.

<!-- s040 -->
An astrolabe. A small one, a seaman's, worn thin at the edge.

<!-- s041 -->
I sat down in another man's house and held it in both hands.

<!-- s042 -->
He had come from the sea and been alone. He had this in his pocket when he came. He had used it, presumably, exactly as I was about to use it, and for the same purpose.

<!-- s043 -->
And then he had died, and somebody had cut his name in a stone.

<!-- s044 -->
I said, out loud, to nobody: "Right. Then you can help me."

<!-- s045 -->
![A disc of brass about the size of my palm, green all over.](media/ch09-b.jpg)

<!-- s046 -->
It took the whole of that winter.

<!-- s047 -->
I cleaned the brass with sand and wet ash. Half the scale was eaten away and I re-cut the missing part by eye, which sounds impossible and is merely slow.

<!-- s048 -->
I took the altitude of the sun at noon on nine clear days and averaged them, because my hands shake and one measurement is a guess. I took the star my father had shown me on a roof in York, and it stood so low over the sea that I had to go up the hill to see it clear of the haze.

<!-- s049 -->
I got my latitude. I am fairly confident of it to this day.

<!-- s050 -->
Longitude I could not get. Nobody could, then. What I had instead was the ship's dead reckoning up to the eleventh day of the storm, and eleven days of being pushed by a wind whose direction I remembered, and a great deal of arithmetic.

<!-- s051 -->
I set it down in columns, in my father's small square hand, on paper I had carried off a wreck.

<!-- s052 -->
And at the end of it I had a direction: a little north of east, and I could put a figure on it, and the figure was probably wrong by ten degrees and possibly by thirty.

<!-- s053 -->
⁂

<!-- s054 -->
I have been asked, since, whether that troubled me. Whether it was worth doing at all, if I could not be sure.

<!-- s055 -->
It did trouble me. For about a week.

<!-- s056 -->
Then I worked out the only answer there is. A man who cannot know is not thereby excused from trying. The trying is done with what he actually has. And if he has done the arithmetic honestly, then the arithmetic is what he owes.

<!-- s057 -->
I drove two posts into the sand outside my door, lined up on my figure, so that I would not have to do it again every morning in the dark.

<!-- s058 -->
They are probably still standing.

<!-- s059 -->
⁂

<!-- s060 -->
The calendar was harder, and I solved it worse.

<!-- s061 -->
I had my post with the notches. I knew exactly how many days I had been on that island — I had cut every one of them, and missed only about a fortnight during the fever, which I later corrected as best I could.

<!-- s062 -->
What I did not have was the other reckoning. The one on the stone. The one the year in Salé had been counted in.

<!-- s063 -->
I knew it went by the moon. I knew a month began when the new moon was seen. I did not know what month it was, and there was nobody within a thousand miles to tell me, and no book.

<!-- s064 -->
So I did the only thing available.

<!-- s065 -->
I began keeping months by the moon, from the first new moon after I decided, and I numbered them. I chose one and kept it as the month of the fast. And I wrote down plainly, on the paper, that I had chosen it and how — so that if I was ever again among people, I could be told how wrong I had been.

<!-- s066 -->
I was probably wrong. I have never found out by how much.

<!-- s067 -->
I kept it for sixteen years.

<!-- s068 -->
⁂

<!-- s069 -->
I will pass over the farming quickly, because Defoe's kind of book would give you forty pages of it and I would rather cut off a finger.

<!-- s070 -->
The grain I thought was rubbish came up in the wet corner where I had shaken out a bag. It took four harvests before I had enough to eat any of it, because for three years I ate none and planted all of it, which was the hardest thing I did on that island, harder than the fever.

<!-- s071 -->
I made bread in the eleventh year. It was bad bread.

<!-- s072 -->
I made pots. It took me two years to make a pot that would hold water, and about two hundred that would not, and when I finally lifted a whole one out of the fire I sat down on the ground and could not speak.

<!-- s073 -->
I got goats. I built a fence badly, and then a better one, and eventually I had milk, and after milk, butter, and I do not think any king in Europe was ever as pleased with anything as I was with butter.

<!-- s074 -->
![Two posts in the sand, so that I would not have to do it again in the dark.](media/ch09-c.jpg)

<!-- s075 -->
By the fifteenth year I had a house dug into a bank, a fenced field, a herd, and a boat I had made and could not launch. I had two posts in the sand outside my door. And I had a headstone up the valley that I visited perhaps four times a year and swept with my hand.

<!-- s076 -->
I want to record something here that will sound like a lie.

<!-- s077 -->
I was happy.

<!-- s078 -->
Not resigned. Not making the best of it. Happy, in a plain daily way that I had never once been in York or London or Brazil, where I had always been on my way to something.

<!-- s079 -->
I had food I had grown, a roof I had cut, an hour I could tell without being told, and a direction I had worked out myself with a dead man's brass.

<!-- s080 -->
I used to think about the boy standing at the rail with salt drying on his face, deciding his father was a frightened old man who had built a wall out of bad luck.

<!-- s081 -->
I would have liked ten minutes with him. I would not have wasted them on advice. I would just have shown him the butter.

<!-- s082 -->
That was the fifteenth year.

<!-- s083 -->
In the sixteenth I went down to the shore on an ordinary morning to look at my boat, and there was a print of a man's naked foot in the sand.
